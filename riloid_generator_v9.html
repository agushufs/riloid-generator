How each pattern is built

Riloid, exactly as in Zanker (2004, eqs 1–3) and Zanker et al. (2003). x and y are in pixels from the top-left corner, and rotation is applied about the image center first.

I(x,y) = 0.5[1 + sin(2π(x − φ(y))/λ)]
φ(y)   = A·sin(2π·y/μ(y))
μ(y)   = μ_top + y(μ_top − μ_bottom)/(y_top − y_bottom)

λ is the grating period. A is the phase-modulation amplitude, a sideways shift in pixels, so the phase amplitude is 2πA/λ rad. μ is the phase-modulation period, shrinking linearly from the top to the bottom edge (y_top = 0, y_bottom = image height). Turning waviness off sets A = 0, a straight grating. Grey levels run from 0 (black) to 1 (white) as in the paper.

Parameters are in output pixels, as in the papers (512 × 512 images), so keep the output size fixed. Zanker (2004) notes that the meaningful quantity is size relative to λ. Riley's Fall seen from 2 m has λ ≈ 0.37° (about 3 cycles/deg).

Radial grating: control pattern shown in Zanker (2004, Fig. 7) and Zanker & Walker (2004, Fig. 5). Zanker describes MacKay's ray pattern with 128 sectors of alternating black and white (n = 64 cycles here). Leviant (1996) describes MacKay's own grating differently: 120 black rays of 1.5°, with white rays of equal width (n = 120; preset provided). The sources disagree on the count and give no other parameters; the Zanker & Walker caption calls the patterns of that figure "smooth intensity gradients ranging between black and white", so the papers do not settle sine versus square wave. Checkerboard with waviness: not in the papers; it applies the riloid phase modulation along both axes.

White-stripe grid on black squares: Radovic & Jablan (2010, pp. 129–130) describe Vasarely's Taymir II (1956) and Supernovae (1959/63) as based on a periodic structure of the simplest kind, a grid of white stripes and a regular system of black squares, and note that it produces small flickering gray dots at the crossings of the white stripes. The paper gives no stripe width, so w is a free parameter. Squares are square (same period λ in x and y). The paper also says Vasarely elaborated this with affine transformations; no mapping is given, so none is implemented.

Kepler/Koffka cube: Radovic & Jablan (2010, p. 128) define the Koffka cube as a cube viewed along its main diagonal: a regular hexagon divided into three rhombi, arranged as the regular tessellation {6,3}. The geometry here follows that definition exactly: hexagons of side λ/√3, each split from its center into three 60°/120° rhombi. The paper gives no luminance values, so the three rhombi take three equally spaced levels (light, middle, dark: +1, 0, −1 of the contrast range). The paper presents antisymmetry as the symmetry of opposites (positive–negative, light–shadow, convex–concave); the reverse-tones option is the positive–negative counterpart (+1, 0, −1 becomes −1, 0, +1). Which rhombus is light, and the choice of three levels, are display choices, not source values.

Concentric rings: Campbell & Robson (in Pirenne et al., 1958) cite Helmholtz's pattern of black and white concentric circles, Leviant (1996) says MacKay also used a concentric grating, and Radovic & Jablan (2010, p. 131) discuss periodic concentric black and white circles. None gives a period or profile, so I = ½[1 + cos(2πr/λ)] is the plain periodic case with λ free. MacKay (1957, Fig. 1) used rings of increasing thickness; that is not periodic and is not implemented because the spacing law is not published there.

Café Wall / Münsterberg: rows of squares in which dark and light bricks alternate, adjacent rows offset horizontally and separated by a thin mortar line; the parallel mortar lines appear tilted. First shown by Fraser (1908) and named by Gregory & Heard (1979); the construction here follows Kitaoka (1998), Kitaoka, Pinna & Brelstaff (2004, Fig. 2A, three tones) and Kitaoka (2007, Fig. 16). With a black line on black and white squares it is the Münsterberg figure (Münsterberg, 1897; Kitaoka, 1998, Fig. 17), which is the case where the mortar level equals the dark-brick level. Per Kitaoka (1998) and Kitaoka et al. (2004), citing Gregory & Heard (1979): the illusion occurs when the mortar luminance lies between the dark and light bricks, or is not much darker than the dark bricks or lighter than the light ones; it disappears when the line is noticeably darker or lighter than both; its size diminishes as the mortar thickens relative to brick height; and the Münsterberg illusion disappears when the line is thicker than about 10 arcmin. "Noticeably" is not quantified in these sources. The sources give no brick size, mortar thickness or tone values, so λ, the mortar thickness and the tone levels are free parameters (brick side = λ/2, squares as in the figures). The row offset is also free: Kitaoka et al. (2004) describe bricks of the same tone in adjacent rows as misaligned by roughly half a brick’s length, so the default is half a brick (λ/4); “roughly” is not quantified, and the papers do not give how the effect varies with the offset (λ/2, a full brick, gives a plain checkerboard). Rows alternate between offset 0 and the chosen offset. This tool draws the geometry only; it does not compute or predict the tilt direction. Not implemented because the papers do not define them numerically: the checkered illusion, shifted gradations, striped cords, Montalvo and the other variants.

Transparency overlay: MacKay (in Pirenne et al., 1958) obtains moiré fringes by covering the stimulus figure with a transparency of itself and displacing it slightly, and Gregory (1994) describes the same for the MacKay rays. Radovic & Jablan (2010, p. 131) describe the layer method, which Vasarely rediscovered in Transparencies. Stacked transparencies multiply transmittance, so the overlay is I′ = I(x, y)·I(x − dx, y − dy) with intensities on a 0–1 scale; the result is darker than either copy. The sources give no displacement size, so d and its direction are free. It works with every pattern type. Rotating the copy is not in these sources and is not offered.

Square-wave edges are antialiased by a linear ramp whose pixel width comes from the analytic phase gradient |∇θ|.
